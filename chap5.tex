\Chapter{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

While this thesis has made substantial progress toward constructing high-dimensional intervals within a coherent inferential framework, further work remains in refining their ideal properties. In particular, for a general dataset it remains unclear how to define and compute the ``correct'' coverage rate for any given parameter. Resolving this question would enable a more principled evaluation of which of the proposed methods achieve the best performance.

This work also opens several avenues for future research in both in false discovery rate (FDR) control and debiased interval construction. \citet{dai2019} developed the PIPE FDR procedure but noted its conservativeness due adjusting for a large number of variables (what we referred to as the full projection based variance). Thus, a Local Quadratic Approximation (LQA) variant provides a compelling alternative. The partial projection based variance of LQA offers the potential to mitigate conservativeness, leading to a more powerful procedure. Another promising direction is the construction of piecewise-scaled parametric null distributions under $\beta_j = 0$, with corresponding p-values that could be used in FDR procedures directly tied back to the penalized point estimates. 

With respect to interval estimation, while the central argument of this thesis has been for biased intervals consistent with the penalized regression assumptions, I have also implemented debiased counterparts. These methods, which omit re-penalization of the Normal likelihood, align naturally with the literature on de-sparsified inference. Although I contend that biased intervals align more faithfully with penalized regression and provide unique insights, I recognize that debiased alternatives may be valuable, and their performance relative to existing approaches remains an important topic for future work.

In this dissertation I argued for intervals that remain consistent with the assumptions that were made when selecting a sparse penalty. Along these lines, I proposed three promising methods and demonstrated how these methods can be extended to other penalties (SCAD, MCP, -Net) and other outcome families (binomial, Poisson). All the methods described in this thesis have been implemented in the \texttt{intervals} function in the \texttt{ncvreg} package, providing comprehensive coverage of outcome-penalty combinations available in the function \texttt{ncvreg}. 

My vision is a future where biased intervals are reported by default alongside biased estimates. I have shown that the common objection --- ``I only care about the coverage of the selected features,'' often made in favor of debiasing procedures --- is more nuanced than anticipated. Preliminary results suggest that biased interval construction may, in fact, offer advantages even in this setting. So, although high-dimensional intervals do not provide traditional frequentist guarantees, they achieve correct average coverage and convey uncertainty about the penalized estimates in ways that debiased methods cannot, ultimately revealing insights that are hidden when one reports estimates alone.

And so, I like to imagine that if the lasso itself could read this thesis, it would close the final chapter with a smile and a quiet sigh: “At last, I have my own intervals.”
