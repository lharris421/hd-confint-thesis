\chapter{Generalized High-Dimensional Intervals for Penalized Regression Models}\label{sec:chap4}

\section{Introduction}

The lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) is a penalized regression method that applies the $L_1$ penalty, $\lambda \sum_{j = 1}^p |\beta_j|$, to a loss function that depends on the outcome of interest where $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter that controls the amount of penalization and $\beta_j$ for $j \in \lbrace 1, \dots, p \rbrace$ are the regression coefficients. The $L_1$ penalty results both in regularization and selection of features. The ability of lasso to perform selection and estimation simultaneously, together with its strong predictive performance in many settings, has led to its widespread use. However, the $L_1$ penalty makes inference challenging because it is not differentiable at zero, violating the smoothness assumptions required in classical likelihood theory. In addition, the estimates are intentionally biased towards zero through shrinkage. To avoid these issues, methods that try to preserve traditional frequentist coverage must attempt to debias and ``de-sparsify'' in one way or another when constructing intervals \citep{ZhangZhang2014, Javanmard2014, Lee2016}. However, in the process of debiasing, the alignment of the confidence intervals to the original lasso estimates is lost.

In earlier work (Chapter 2 and Chapter 3), we put forth an alternative framework that allows intervals to be centered on the lasso point estimates. Thoughts on: This general approach has three variants (RL-P, PIPE-P, and LQA-P) and focuses on average rather than individual coverage. This framework produces intervals that are biased like the point estimates and leads to inference that is in alignment with the original assumptions used in fitting the lasso. Intervals constructed in this manner have properties that lie somewhere between those of confidence and credible intervals, and we refer to the resulting intervals as high-dimensional intervals (HDIs).

While the methods proposed so far have focused on lasso penalized linear regression, the concepts apply to other sparse penalties and other outcome types; this is the topic of the current manuscript. Of specific interest is the extension to penalties such as SCAD \citep{Fan2001} and MCP \citep{Zhang2010} which introduce less bias than the lasso. As a result, the corresponding biased SCAD and MCP intervals may provide a middle ground between biased lasso intervals and debiased lasso intervals while still producing intervals and point estimates which are aligned with each other. In addition, we extend the methods to -Net type penalties as well as to Poisson and logistic penalized regression.

Efficiently computing intervals for these extensions in high dimensions, however, is not straightforward, as it involves calculation of quantiles from nonstandard distributions. However, we show that these distributions can be decomposed in a piecewise manner into scaled parametric distributions. This decomposition allows HDIs to be computed efficiently without the need for numerical integration.

In Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal} we provide general details on how to analytically compute quantiles from piecewise-scaled parametric distributions in order to construct HDIs, using the Laplace-Normal (for the lasso) as an example. In Section~\ref{sec:review} we review the details specific to the three methods introduced in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 in greater generality. In Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing}, we give the piecewise decomposed posteriors for SCAD and MCP and in Section~\ref{sec:enet} we show how the -Net penalties work into this framework. Then, in Section~\ref{sec:alt-fam}, we extend the methods to logistic and Poisson penalized regression. All the extensions can be combined with minimal effort, but specific details will be noted where necessary and an example application combining extensions is provided in Supplement~\ref{sup:multiple}. Throughout, results will be shown for PIPE-P for the sake of clarity while results for LQA-P are provided in Supplement~\ref{sup:lqa} and results for RL-P are provided in Supplement~\ref{sup:rlp}.

\section{Piecewise-Scaled Parametric Posteriors}
\label{sec:laplace-normal}

The ability to represent penalized regression as a Bayesian posterior is central to the construction of high-dimensional intervals. To review the setup for linear regression lasso, note that the lasso can be formulated as a Bayesian regression model with a Laplace (double exponential) prior and a Normal likelihood \citep{Park2008}. In Chapter 2, we used this fact to construct intervals for the lasso from the quantiles of Laplace-Normal distributions for each $\beta_j$. In Chapter 3, modifications were introduced to the likelihood in order to center the intervals on the lasso point estimates. The extensions that follow either define an alternative prior (alternate penalties) or how to adjust the Normal likelihood (alternative outcomes).

The resulting posterior distributions do not have a standard parametric form, but we show here that the quantiles can still be computed analytically. We use the Laplace-Normal distribution as a example, but the process may also be applies to the posteriors that result from the penalties in Sections~\ref{sec:bias_reducing}~and~\ref{sec:enet}.

Consider a Normal likelihood with location $\mu$ and scale $s$:
$$ L(\theta | \mu, s^2) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s^2}(\theta - \mu)^2\right) $$

\noindent along with a Laplace prior on $\theta$:
$$p(\theta) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\lam}{s^2} \abs{\theta}\right).$$

\noindent We can decompose the resulting Laplace-Normal distribution into two pieces:
\as{
p(\theta | \mu, s^2) 
&\propto
\begin{cases}
C_{1} \exp(-\frac{1}{2s^2} (\theta_j - (\mu + \lambda))^2), \text{ if } \theta < 0, \\
C_{2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2s^2} (\theta_j - (\mu - \lambda))^2), \text{ if } \theta \geq 0 \\
\end{cases}
}

\noindent where $C_{1} = \exp(\mu \lam / s^2)$ and $C_{2} = \exp(- \mu \lam / s^2)$. The piecewise distribution is made up of two scaled truncated Normal distributions. 

Figure~\ref{fig:1-man3} illustrates the process of computing quantiles for a simple example with $\mu = 1$, $s^2 = 1$, and $\lam = 0.5$. The black curve is the the posterior and the goal is to compute the 95\% HDI (black dashed lines) corresponding to probabilities of 0.025 and 0.975 (gray shaded areas). Focusing on the lower bound (top), this is done by mapping $p = 0.025$ to a corresponding $p_*$ (blue shaded area) for the underlying (unscaled) blue Normal distribution, where the CDF can be inverted to compute the quantile.

    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figure1-man3}
    \caption{\label{fig:1-man3} A depiction of the Laplace-Normal distribution (black) and how it relates to two underlying Normal distributions (red and blue). Additionally, shows the 95\% HDI (vertical black dashed lines). Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal} provides a full description of how the underlying Normal distributions are used in the computation of the quantiles for the Laplace-Normal.}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

The general outline for mapping computing a quantile, $q$, using the underlying parametric distributions is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Decompose the posterior piecewise, simplifying each component to a standard parametric form and a multiplicative constant
  \item Compute the total probability in each of the underlying parametric distributions over their respective support
  \item Compute the total probability in each interval of the posterior
  \item Determine which interval the quantile resides in, then map $p$ to $p_*$ for an underlying parametric distribution, and invert the CDF to compute the quantile
\end{enumerate}

We now describe this process in more detail; for simplicity, we describe the process on the standard scale although in practice, all computations should use the log scale for numerical stability. Let $K$ be the number of pieces (here $K = 2$) for the decomposed posterior and let $I_i=[\ell_i,u_i]$ be the support for piece $i$. The pieces are numbered from left to right based on their support. Let $F_i(x)$ and $F_i^{-1}(x)$ be the CDF and its inverse for the $i^{th}$ underlying parametric distribution. On their respective support, let the total probability in the underlying unscaled distributions be $U_i = F_i(u_i) - F_i(\ell_i)$ and denote the normalizing constants for the underlying distributions as $\tilde{C}_i$. Note that if $\tilde{C}_i$ is the same for all $i$, these normalizing constants can be ignored (this is the case with the Laplace-Normal but not for SCAD and MCP). Finally, let the probability in each of the corresponding components of the Laplace-Normal be denoted $P_i$, which can be computed as follows. Let $r_i = \log(C_iU_i\tilde{C}_i^{-1})$ and $m = \max\{r_i: i = 1, \ldots, K\}$, then $c = m + \log(\sum\exp(r_i - m))$ and $P_i = \exp(r_i - c)$. Note that $\sum P_i = 1$. Using $P_i$, we can determine which piece $q$ comes from and mark the corresponding components with a $*$ by setting $* = \min\{i : p < \sum_{j=1}^i P_j\}$.

Then, the transformation to map $p$ to $p_*$ is: 
$$
p_* = \frac{U_{*}}{P_{*}} (p - \sum_{j = 1}^{{*} - 1} P_j) + F_{*}(\ell_*).
$$

The sum accounts for the probability that has already accumulated in earlier pieces of the posterior, while the weight $U_{*} / P_{*}$ indicates how probability from the underlying distribution is reweighted relative to its probability under the posterior over support $I_*$. Together, these tell how much probability is needed in the underlying distribution to get to the desired quantile. However, we need to accumulate this probability starting at $\ell_{*}$, so the $F_{*}(\ell_*)$ term is required to account for this shift. Once $p_*$ has been computed, then $q = F_*^{-1}(p_*)$.

For the Laplace-Normal example here, the underlying Normal distributions are $\Norm(1 \pm 0.5, 1)$ and
$
U_1 = 0.067,
U_2 = 0.691,
P_1 = 0.208, \text{ and }
P_2 = 0.792.
$
So, for the lower bound (top Figure~\ref{fig:1-man3}), $p = 0.025 < P_1 = 0.208$ so $* = 1$ and the weight, $U_{*}/P_{*} = 0.322$. The sum is $0$ and since $\ell_* = - \infty$, $F_{*}(\ell_*)$ is also $0$. So, $p_* = 0.322 \times 0.025 = 0.008$ (blue shaded area) and $q = F_*^{-1}(0.008) = -0.909$. For the upper bound (bottom Figure~\ref{fig:1-man3}), $p = 0.975$ which is larger than $P_1$ but of course not larger than $P_1 + P_2 = 1$, so $* = 2$. For this region, the weight, $U_{*}/P_{*} = 0.872$. However here, $\sum_{j = 1}^{{*} - 1} P_j = P_1 = 0.208$ and $F_{*}(\ell_*) = F_{*}(0) = 0.309$. So, $p_* = 0.872 \times (0.975 - 0.208) + 0.309 = 0.978$ (red shaded area) and $q = F_*^{-1}(0.978) = 2.514$. Note that all quantile calculations are taken with respect to a Gaussian distribution $F_*$ for which efficient algorithms exist.

Computing a quantile from any piecewise-scaled parametric distribution is outlined in Algorithm~\ref{alg:quantile-general}. The numbers in the comments correspond to the outline above.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Quantile of a Piecewise-Scaled Parametric Distribution}
\label{alg:quantile-general}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require
${(C_i,\tilde{C}_i,F_i,F_i^{-1},I_i=[\ell_i,u_i])}_{i=1}^K, p\in(0,1)$
\Statex
\State // 2. Compute total probability in underlying parametric distributions
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $U_i \gets F_i(u_i) - F_i(\ell_i)$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State // 3. Compute posterior piece probabilities via log-sum-exp
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $r_i \gets \log C_i + \log U_i - \log \tilde{C}_i$
\EndFor
\State $m \gets \max\{r_i: i = 1, \ldots, K\}$
\State $c \gets m + \log\left(\sum_{i=1}^K \exp(r_i - m)\right)$
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $P_i \gets \exp(r_i - c)$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State // 4. Locate active piece for probability $p$, map onto the underlying CDF of piece $*$, invert
\State $* \gets \min\{i : p < \sum_{j=1}^i P_j\}$
\State $w_* \gets U_{*} / P_{*}$ 
\State $p_* \gets w_* (p - \sum_{j = 1}^{{*} - 1} P_j) + F_{*}(\ell_{*})$
\State $q \gets F_{*}^{-1}(p_*)$
\State \Return $q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Likelihood adjustments}
\label{sec:review}

Before outlining the extensions, we review in general the three previously introduced adjustments to the likelihood, specifically how they respectively set $\mu$ and $s^2$ (the mean and variance) in the Normal likelihoods. While the penalized regression objective does imply a likelihood, the high-dimensional nature of the parameter space introduces issues --- when constructing an interval for $\beta_j$, all other coefficients act as nuisance parameters. The adjustments vary in how they handle the nuisance parameters in the construction of the Normal likelihood. Although the adjustments were introduced with lasso, they can be extended to other penalties and so we present the generalized form here. However, the form presented is specific to linear regression but is extended to logistic and Poisson regression in Section~\ref{sec:alt-fam}.

Let $\X$ be an $n \times p$ matrix of features and $\y$ be a length $n$ vector of independent outcomes. Throughout, $\X$ is standardized so that $\x_j \Tr \x_j = n$. Additionally, let $\bbh$ be the estimates for $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ using penalized regression at the value of $\lam$ which minimizes cross validation error. Let $\hat{S} = \lbrace k: \bh_k \neq  0 \rbrace$ denote the set of selected features. Then, let $\hat{S}_j$ denote the set of selected features that excludes feature $j$, so that $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} \text{ if } j \notin \hat{S}$ and $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} - \lbrace j \rbrace \text{ if } j \in \hat{S}$. Additionally, let $\r_j = \y - \X_{-j}\bbh_{-j}$ be the partial residuals obtained by excluding feature $j$. The distinguishing features between the three adjustments are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:review}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Projection} & \textbf{Mean} \\
\midrule

PIPE-P & $\Q_{\hat{S}_j} = \I - \X_{\hat{S}_j}(\X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr \X_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr$ & $z_j = \tfrac{1}{n}\x_j \Tr\r_{-j}$ \\

The likelihood variance estimate for for RL-P and PIPE-P is $\hat{\sigma}^2 / \x_j \Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \x \Tr$ and for LQA-P is $\hat{\sigma}^2 / \x_j \Tr \tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j} \x \Tr$ where in both cases $\hat{\sigma}^2$, the error variance, is estimated using the suggestion of \cite{Reid2016} with $\hat{\sigma}^2 = ||\y - \X\bbh||_2^2 / (n - |\hat{S}_{\CV}|)$, where $|\hat{S}_{\CV}|$ = $\sum \left( \bbh \neq 0 \right)$.  

In summary, LQA-P and PIPE-P share the same likelihood mean while PIPE-P and RL-P share the same likelihood variance. The likelihood mean for PIPE-P and LQA-P is based on a partial projection onto the active features (i.e., uses the shrunken estimates) whereas the mean for RL-P is based on a full projection onto the active features. Meanwhile, the likelihood variance for PIPE-P and RL-P is calculated based on a full projection onto the active features whereas for LQA-P it is based on a partial projection. Note that the alternative mean used by LQA-P and PIPE-P results in intervals that are centered on corresponding point estimates.

\section{Bias Reducing Penalties (MCP and SCAD)}
\label{sec:bias_reducing}

A common argument against intervals that have correct average coverage but whose individual coverage varies is that the variables that are potentially of the greatest interest (those with the largest effect) are the ones that have the lowest coverage. However, this pattern arises from the bias introduced by the penalty, not from a flaw in the interval methods themselves.

Improving the coverage for larger coefficients can be accomplished instead by using an alternative penalty. Two penalties that we discuss here are the Smoothly Clipped Absolute Deviation (SCAD) penalty and the Minimax Concave Penalty (MCP). The SCAD penalty, introduced by \cite{Fan2001}, is a concave function that heavily shrinks small coefficients while leaving sufficiently large ones essentially unpenalized, yielding an estimator with the oracle property. The MCP penalty, proposed by \cite{Zhang2010}, uses a minimax-concave piecewise quadratic-linear form, which produces nearly unbiased coefficient estimates and consistent variable selection under mild conditions. Both penalties have a tuning parameter, $\gamma$, which controls the amount of curvature. The SCAD and MCP penalties are defined as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{SCAD \;(\(\gamma>2\))} &:\qquad
\displaystyle
p_\lambda\!\bigl(|\beta|;\,\lambda,\gamma\bigr)=
\begin{cases}
\lambda|\beta|, &
0 \le |\beta| \le \lambda, \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2\gamma\lambda|\beta| - |\beta|^{2} - \lambda^{2}}
      {2(\gamma-1)}, &
\lambda < |\beta| < \gamma\lambda, \\[10pt]
\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(\gamma+1)}{2}, &
|\beta| \ge \gamma\lambda
\end{cases} \\[12pt]
\textbf{MCP \;(\(\gamma>1\))} &:\qquad
\displaystyle
p_\lambda\!\bigl(|\beta|;\,\lambda,\gamma\bigr)=
\begin{cases}
\lambda|\beta| - \dfrac{|\beta|^{2}}{2\gamma}, &
0 \le |\beta| \le \gamma\lambda, \\[6pt]
\dfrac{\gamma\lambda^{2}}{2}, &
|\beta| \ge \gamma\lambda.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$


\noindent Both SCAD and MCP are single-stage approaches that taper off penalization as $\beta$ increases in magnitude. 

Figure~\ref{fig:4-man3} displays what these penalties look like in comparison to the lasso penalty. For SCAD, $\gam$  is set to be $3.7$ and for MCP $\gam$ is set to be $3$, which are both commonly used defaults in practice. The same value of $\lam$ is used for all three penalties for consistency, however, the $\lam$ used for each penalty in practice generally differs even when applied to the same dataset.


\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figure3-man3}
    \caption{\label{fig:4-man3} A depiction of the lasso, MCP, and SCAD penalties as a function of $\beta$. For all 3 penalties, $\lam = 1$. For MCP, $\gamma = 3$ and for SCAD, $\gamma = 3.7$.}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

These penalties are often referred to as non-convex penalties with $\gam$ determining the rate the penalty tapers off at (and consequently, the concavity). Both penalties start out by applying the same rate of penalization as the lasso but then smoothly relax the penalization rate to zero as the magnitude of the estimate increases. The main difference between MCP and SCAD is that MCP relaxes the penalization immediately while for SCAD the penalization rate remains flat until the estimate exceeds $\lam$.

\subsection{Overview}

Computation of the intervals for SCAD and MCP proceed exactly as described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:quantile-general}. What remains to be worked out is the piecewise distributions. Here, as in Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal}, the details are provided in general for any Normal likelihood.

Priors corresponding to penalties are proportional to $\exp(-p_\lam(\theta) / s^2)$, where $p_\lam(\theta)$ is the respective penalty. This leads to a piecewise decomposed posterior for SCAD of

\[
p(\theta \mid \mu, s^2)
\;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
  C_{1}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta < -\,\gamma\lambda,\\[2ex]

  C_{2}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu + \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
            {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
  \Bigg),
  & -\gamma\lambda \le \theta < -\lambda,\\[3ex]

  C_{3}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-(\mu+\lambda))^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & -\lambda \le \theta < 0,\\[2ex]

  C_{4}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-(\mu-\lambda))^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & 0 \le \theta \le \lambda,\\[2ex]

  C_{5}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu - \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
            {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
  \Bigg),
  & \lambda < \theta \le \gamma\lambda,\\[3ex]

  C_{6}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta > \gamma\lambda,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\text{with constants}\qquad
\begin{aligned}
C_{1} = C_{6}
&= \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\gamma+1)\lambda^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1ex]
C_{2}
&= \exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{\displaystyle \Big(\dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu + \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
         {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
    - \frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}
    + \frac{\lambda^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}
  \Bigg),\\[2ex]
C_{3}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{(\mu+\lambda)^2 - \mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\qquad
C_{4}
= \exp\!\Big(\frac{(\mu-\lambda)^2 - \mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1ex]
C_{5}
&= \exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{\displaystyle \Big(\dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu - \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
         {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
    - \frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}
    + \frac{\lambda^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}
  \Bigg).
\end{aligned}
\]

\noindent For MCP the simplified piecewise distribution is

\[
p(\theta \mid \mu, s^2)
\;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
  C_{1}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta < -\,\gamma\lambda,\\[2.0ex]

  C_{2}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\,\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(\mu+\lambda)\Big)^{\!2}}
             {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}
  \Bigg),
  & -\gamma\lambda \le \theta < 0,\\[3.0ex]

  C_{3}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\,\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(\mu-\lambda)\Big)^{\!2}}
             {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}
  \Bigg),
  & 0 \le \theta \le \gamma\lambda,\\[3.0ex]

  C_{4}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta > \gamma\lambda,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\text{with constants}\qquad
\begin{aligned}
C_{1} = C_4
&= \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\gamma\,\lambda^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1.0ex]
C_{2}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{\gamma\,(\mu+\lambda)^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}\;-\;\frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1.0ex]
C_{3}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{\gamma\,(\mu-\lambda)^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}\;-\;\frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big).
\end{aligned}
\]

For this figure, $\lam = 0.5$, $s^2 = 1$, and $\gamma = 3$. This figures highlights that the distribution can be decomposed into 4 pieces from 3 scaled Normal distributions. The solid portion of each scaled Normal density is what is ``used'' in the posterior whereas the dotted portion is the ``unused'' portion.

\subsection{Results}

SCAD and MCP both introduce less bias than the lasso, to see how this translates into practice, we apply each of the penalties to a dataset on acute respiratory illness collected by the World Health Organization (WHO/ARI) \citep{Harrell1998}. The WHO/ARI Multicentre Study collected data on several acute respiratory illnesses in multiple countries. Here, we analyze a subset of this study, concerning 816 infants who presented with symptoms of pneumonia in Ethiopia, a major cause of morbidity and mortality for infants under 3 months of age. Our goal here is to identify risk factors for increased severity among infants presenting with serious infections. The outcome is ordinal (taking on a number from 1 - 5); however, for simplicity we treat the outcome as following a Gaussian distribution. The variables collected contain information on vital signs, family history, and clinical observations and represent a range of data types from binary to ordinal to continuous. With $n \approx 10p$, this dataset is not high dimensional. However, sparsity is beneficial both for interpretation and for the practical implementation of using the resulting fit in clinical practice. The 80\% HDIs for PIPE-P using lasso, MCP, and SCAD along with correspond point estimates are provided in Figure~\ref{fig:5-man3} for the variables which were selected by at least one of the penalties.

We see the hallmark of the non-convex penalties, the larger non-zero estimates --- and corresponding intervals as a result --- are less pulled toward $0$ for MCP and SCAD. Otherwise, the intervals are generally comparable.

\subsection{Revisiting an argument for biased intervals}
\label{sec:bias_v_debias}

The introduction of bias reducing penalties brings in an interesting addition to the biased versus debiased interval discussion. With SCAD and MCP, there is perhaps less of a benefit to debiasing as such penalties attempt to introduce the minimum amount of bias necessary. To this end, consider the following simulation comparing PIPE-P with lasso, SCAD, and MCP penalties to debiased methods. Data was generated with $n = 50$, $\boldsymbol{X} \iid \Norm(0,1)$, and $\y = \boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon \iid \Norm(0, 1)$. $\bb$ was generated under 4 different setups (noted below) and each Scenario was ran 1000 times. Results are provided in Table~\ref{tab:1-man3}. The coverages and widths in the table are for the features selected by the respective penalties in each iteration of the simulation. We refer to this coverage as the relevant average coverage as it focuses on the features which are presumably of greatest interest to the practitioner: those selected by the respective penalty. Iterations where the penalty selected no variables are excluded from the table.


\paragraph{$\bb$ setups:}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item $p = 16$: $4$ large signals ($\beta = \pm 5$)
  \item $p = 100$: $4$ large signals ($\beta = \pm 5$)

\section{-Net Penalties}
\label{sec:enet}

Proposed by \cite{zou2005regularization}, the Elastic Net blends lasso's sparsity with Ridge's shrinkage to retain groups of highly correlated predictors while controlling estimation variability. The Elastic Net penalty is a mixture of $L_2$ (ridge) and $L_1$ (lasso) penalties:
$$
p_\lam(\bb | \lam, \alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha) \lam }{2} ||\bb||^2_2 + \alpha \lam ||\bb||_1,
$$

\noindent where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ controls the tradeoff between the two components. When $\alpha = 1$, the penalty becomes the lasso penalty and when $\alpha = 0$, the penalty becomes the ridge penalty. The main reason one may choose to use Elastic Net over the lasso is that the addition of the ridge penalty can add stability. So while the previous methods in Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing} focused on reducing bias, here the addition of the $L_2$ component reduces the variability compared to the lasso estimates. Furthermore, while the lasso solutions are not always unique, Elastic Net solutions are, a quality inherited from the ridge penalty. However, as mentioned, Elastic Net still results in sparsity, a quality inherited from the lasso.

The Ridge penalty can also be applied to the nonconvex penalties. Actually, there may be an even stronger motivation as the objective functions for MCP and SCAD may fail to be convex (resulting in multiple local minima). Introducing a Ridge penalty (which is strictly convex) can help improve numerical stability.

So, the general form for -Net type penalties is

$$
p_\lam(\bb | \lam, \alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha) \lam }{2} ||\bb||^2_2 + p_{\lam}(\bb | \alpha\lam, \ldots),
$$

\noindent where $p_{\lam}(\bb | \alpha\lam, \ldots)$ is the penalty for one of the previously described sparse penalties (lasso, SCAD, MCP) but with $\lam$ adjusted based on the relative contribution of that sparse penalty. 

\subsection{Overview}

The prior corresponding to -Net penalty for a single $\beta_j$ is

$$
\propto \exp(-\frac{(1-\alpha) \lam}{2s^2}\beta_j^2)\exp(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \alpha\lam, \ldots)),
$$

\noindent With this prior, the posterior can be generally written as

\as{
p(\beta | \mu, s^2) &\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s^2}(\beta_j - \mu)^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\alpha) \lam}{2s^2}\beta_j^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \alpha\lam, \ldots)\right) \\
&\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1 + (1-\alpha)\lam}{2s^2}(\beta_j - \frac{\mu}{1 + (1 - \alpha)\lam})^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \alpha\lam, \ldots)\right).
}

\noindent That is, the Ridge portion of the prior can be absorbed into the Normal likelihood resulting in an updated mean and variance. From here, the process proceeds along the lines of the respective sparse penalty in Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing}.

\subsection{Results}

We would expect the Elastic Net intervals to have behavior that falls into a middle ground between Ridge Posterior intervals and PIPE Posterior intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:6-man3} provides a comparison between intervals produced by Ridge Posterior, those produced by lasso (PIPE-P), and those produced by Elastic Net (PIPE-P with $\alpha = 0.5$). A single dataset was generated with $n = p = 100$, $\beta_{A} = 1$ and $\beta_{B}, \beta_{N1}, \ldots, \beta_{N98} = 0$, $\cor(\x_{A}, \x_{B}) = .99$, while all of the N (noise) variables are uncorrelated with $A$, $B$, and each other, $\X \iid \Norm(0,1)$, and $\y = \X\bb + \epsilon$, $\epsilon \iid \Norm(0,1)$. This simulation setup is illuminating in understand how different methods handle uncertainty, here in the form of high correlation between two predictors.

    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figure5-man3}
    \caption{\label{fig:6-man3} Provides results for the dataset described in Section~\ref{sec:enet}. Shows a single example of intervals produced by Ridge Posterior (left), Elastic Net (using PIPE-P, middle), and lasso (right) for the first 20 variables.}

The stability introduced by the ridge penalty reduces the selection-driven polarization in interval widths compared to using the lasso alone, yielding consistency in width between features A and B. This occurs because with Elastic Net, highly correlated features such as $A$ and $B$ are almost always selected together. So whereas for Ridge Posterior both intervals are narrow and for PIPE-P just the interval for B is wide since just A is selected, Elastic Net attributes equal uncertainty to A and B --- like Ridge Posterior -- but in a way that reflects the high correlation between A and B --- like PIPE-P.

It should be noted that Ridge Posterior serves as a general reference here. Even if $\alpha$ is near 0, the resulting intervals for Elastic Net with PIPE-P will be wide compared to the Ridge Posterior intervals. This occurs because PIPE-P is still using a full projection based variance. As $\alpha$ is decreased towards $0$ the impact of the penalty will resemble the Ridge penalty, but the variance will be heavily inflated due to the lack of sparsity. On the other hand, LQA-P intervals (Supplement~\ref{sup:lqa}) have more similar behavior to Ridge Posterior.

\section{Generalized Linear Models}
\label{sec:alt-fam}

The framework described in this work generalizes naturally to generalized linear models. In this section, we extend the methods to penalized logistic and Poisson regression by detailing the necessary adjustments to both the likelihood and the prior. Let $\boldsymbol{\eta} = \beta_0 + \X \bb$ and $\eta_i$ be the $i^{th}$ element of $\boldsymbol{\eta}$, The penalized objectives are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Logistic:}\quad
Q_{\text{bern}}(\bb\mid\X,\y,\lambda)
  &= \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
     \Bigl[
       \log\!\bigl(1+e^{\eta_i}\bigr)
       - y_i\eta_i
     \Bigr]
     + p_\lam(\bb|\lam, \ldots), \\[6pt]
\text{Poisson:}\quad
Q_{\text{pois}}(\bb\mid\X,\y,\lambda)
  &= \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
     \Bigl[
       e^{\eta_i}
       - y_i\eta_i
     \Bigr]
     + p_\lam(\bb|\lam, \ldots).
\end{aligned}
$$

\subsection{Overview}

Many of the details for fitting penalized regression to generalized outcomes are what are needed to extend the methods. Coordinate descent algorithms for getting estimates for Poisson and logistic penalized regression use Taylor series expansions to obtain quadratic approximations to the loss function and then proceed with the same algorithm used for linear penalized regression. Regardless of the family, the general form for the Taylor series approximated loss is as follows.

$$
L(\bb|\X,\y) \approx \tfrac{1}{2n} (\tilde{\y} - \boldsymbol{\eta}) \Tr \W (\tilde{\y} - \boldsymbol{\eta}),
$$

\noindent where $\tilde{\y} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}} + \W^{-1}(\y - \hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})$. What changes from family to family is just $\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$ and $\W$ which for binomial and Poisson families are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\pi}_i &=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{e^{\hat{\eta}_i}}{1+e^{\hat{\eta}_i}}
  & \text{(Binomial)}\\[10pt]
\displaystyle
e^{\hat{\eta}_i}
  & \text{(Poisson)}
\end{cases}
\qquad\text{ and } \qquad
\mathbf W &=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\hat{\pi}_i(1-\hat{\pi}_i)\bigr)
  & \text{(Binomial)}\\[10pt]
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\hat{\pi}_i\bigr)
  & \text{(Poisson)}
\end{cases}.
\end{aligned}
$$


\noindent This process can be applied to other GLMs by deriving their respective values for $\hat{\pi}_i$ and $\W$. The objective function resembles that of linear penalized regression, but with observational weights, $\W$. 

Accordingly, $\tilde{z}_j = \frac{1}{n}\x_j \Tr\boldsymbol{W} (\tilde{\y} -  \X_{-j} \bbh_{-j})$ is the weighted regression of the partial pseudo residuals $(\tilde{\y} -  \X_{-j} \bbh_{-j})$ onto $\x_j$. Conditional on $\bb_{-j} = \bbh_{-j}$, the mean for $\beta_j$ used in PIPE-P and LQA-P adjustments is $\tilde{z}_j / \nu_j$ where $\nu_j = \frac{1}{n} \x \Tr_j \boldsymbol{W} \x_j$. The projection matrices are also weighted. Let $\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} = \W^{1/2} \X_{\hat{S}_j}$. Then the full projection matrix onto the active set (for PIPE-P and RL-P) is
$$
\Q_{\hat{S}_j} = \I - \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j}(\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr.
$$

\noindent Of course, unlike the gaussian case, we do not have an error variance as the variance is not independent of the mean. So, for feature $j$, the variance used in the Normal likelihood is $(\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2}\x_j)^{-1}$. For LQA-P, $\tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j}$ is used instead of $\Q_{\hat{S}_j}$ where
$$\tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j} = \I_n - \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j}(\tfrac{1}{n}\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} + \P\W^{*-1})^{-1}\tfrac{1}{n}\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr.$$

\noindent To ensure the modes match the corresponding point estimates, $\lam$ must also be scaled for each parameter:
$$
\lambda_j = \frac{\lambda}{\nu_j}.
$$

\noindent However, for -Net type penalties, scaling is not applied to the ridge portion. That is, letting $\lam_1 = \alpha \lam$ and $\lam_2$ = $(1-\alpha)\lam$, then 
$$
\lambda_{1,j} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\nu_j},
$$

\noindent and $\lam_2$ is left alone.

Lastly, for RL-P recall that the mean used by differs from that of PIPE-P and LQA-P and for generlized penalized regression the mean is
$$
\tilde{\beta}_j = (\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2}\x_j)^{-1}(\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2} \tilde{\y}).
$$

With these pieces in place, the process to compute intervals proceeds as usual.


\subsection{Results}

The coverage patterns for binary and count outcomes are similar to the pattern for gaussian outcomes: higher coverage near zero, lower coverage away from zero, and average coverage converging to the nominal rate. Examples for logistic and Poisson penalized regression are provided in Figure~\ref{fig:3-man3}. Each data set was simulated as follows. The elements of $\X_{n \times p}$ were generated independently from a $\Norm(0, 1)$ with $p = 101$ and four different values of $n$: 50, 100, 400, and 1000. Additionally, $\bb$ was set to equally spaced quantiles of a Laplace distribution. The coefficients were then scaled so that the latent signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) equals 1. Finally, $\y$ was generated as $\y = \Pois(\exp(\X\bb))$ for the Poisson outcome and $\y = \Bern(\logit^{-1}(\X\bb))$ for a binary outcome. For both Poisson and logistic penalized regression, we generated 1000 independent data sets for each value of n; for each data set, PIPE-P intervals with a lasso penalty were constructed and coverage determined. The dashed lines represent the average coverage for each method across all coefficients, while the solid lines are smoothed estimates of coverage as a function of $\beta$.

While the overall coverage patterns for binary and count outcomes are similar to the coverage pattern for gaussian outcomes, they differ by outcome type due to different likelihood variance calculations and the relative amounts of information per observation in the different outcome types. For logistic and Poisson regression, the variance is not modeled independent of the mean, and, as such, the computation of the likelihood variance differs by outcome type. For a gaussian outcome, the method recommended by \cite{Reid2016} is used to estimate error variance which is then used in the estimate for the likelihood variance. For Poisson and logistic penalized regression, the estimate of likelihood variance is instead $(\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2}\x_j)^{-1}$. And, while the form is the same for logistic and Poisson penalized regression, they of course differ due to their respective $\W$ matrices. Lastly, it is well known that the amount of information per n is lower for binary outcomes which likely accounts for the lower coverage for larger coefficient values and the wider window of over coverage around zero compared to the Poisson outcome at the same values of n.

\section{Discussion}

The extensions in this manuscript share the same objective as the original methods: to produce a single, coherent set of coefficient estimates, predictions, and intervals. The extensions are implemented in the function \texttt{intervals} as part of the \texttt{ncvreg} package and provide full coverage for the range of outcome / penalty combinations available when using \texttt{ncvreg()} to fit a model.

In total, there are over 50 combinations of penalties and likelihood adjustments. While we haven't looked at every combination in detail, in our experience, each combination has behaved as expected based on our understanding of the penalty and the adjustment, all of which speaks to the coherence of the proposed framework.
