\chapter{Extending Laplace-Normal Quantile Computation}\label{sec:chap4}

\section{Introduction}

The lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) is a penalized regression method that applies the $L_1$ penalty, $\lambda \sum_{j = 1}^p |\beta_j|$, to a loss function that depends on the outcome of interest where $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter that controls the amount of penalization and $\beta_j$ for $j \in \lbrace 1, \dots, p \rbrace$ are the regression coefficients. The $L_1$ penalty results both in regularization and selection of features. The ability of lasso to perform selection and estimation simultaneously as well as the predictive ability imparted by regularization has led to its widespread usage. However, the $L_1$ penalty makes inference for the lasso difficult as the lasso is singular at the origin and lacks a second order derivative. This is in addition to the fact that the estimates are intentionally biased which is an issue for obtaining intervals with traditional frequentist coverage. As such, methods targeting traditional frequentist coverage must attempt to debias in one way or another during intervals construction, essentially circumnavigating the issues introduced by the lasso penalty \citep{ZhangZhang2014, Javanmard2014, Lee2016}. However, in the process of debiasing, the alignment of the confidence intervals to the original lasso estimates is lost.

Alternatively, Chapter 2 suggested a framework to interval construction that puts a priority on constructing intervals more consistent with the lasso point estimates. This general approach, and the corresponding method Relaxed Lasso Posterior (RL-P), focuses on average rather than individual coverage. Doing so produces intervals that are biased just like the point estimates, but also leads to inference that is in better alignment with the original assumptions used in fitting the lasso. Intervals constructed in this manner have properties that are a mix of confidence and credible intervals, and we refer to the resulting intervals as high-dimensional intervals (HDIs).

RL-P constructs intervals from Laplace-Normal distributions by leveraging the Bayesian formulation of the lasso penalty as a Laplace prior. However, in severe cases, RL-P intervals do not contain the corresponding lasso point estimates. So, in Chapter 3, we built on this framework with the added constraint that HDIs are centered on the corresponding lasso point estimates. Along these lines, we introduced two alternative methods PIPE Posterior (PIPE-P) and Local Quadratic Approximation Posterior (LQA-P) which use the same construction as RL-P, but introduce alternative likelihood constructions that result in distributions where the lasso point estimates are the modes.

While the methods proposed so far have been focused on lasso penalized linear regression, the general concepts carry over to other sparse penalties and other outcome types; this is the topic of the current manuscript. Of specific interest is the extension to penalties such as SCAD \citep{Fan2001} and MCP \citep{Zhang2010} which introduce less bias in comparison to the lasso. As a result, the corresponding biased SCAD and MCP intervals may provide a middle ground between biased lasso intervals and debiased lasso intervals by producing intervals and point estimates that align with each other while minimizing the bias introduced. In addition, we extend the methods to -Net type penalties as well as to Poisson and logistic penalized regression.

All extensions in this manuscript are applicable to the previously proposed methods, as they all rely on the formulation of the penalty as a prior to construct distributions viewed as Bayesian posteriors which are defined piecewise using scaled parametric distributions, which we refer to as piecewise-scaled parametric distributions. We will revisit the details, but it is these distributions that are leveraged to compute HDIs. Throughout, results will be shown for the PIPE-P method for the sake of clarity while results for LQA-P are provided in \ref{sup:lqa}.

In Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal} we provide general details on how to analytically compute quantiles from piecewise-scaled parametric distributions in order to construct HDIs, using the Laplace-Normal as an example. In Section~\ref{sec:review} we review the details specific to the three methods introduced in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 in greater generality. In Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing}, we give the piecewise defined posteriors for SCAD and MCP and in Section~\ref{sec:enet} we show how the -Net penalties work into this framework. Then, in Section~\ref{sec:alt-fam}, we extend the methods to logistic and Poisson penalized regression. All the extensions can be combined with minimal effort, but specific details will be noted where necessary and an example application is provided in \ref{sup:multiple}.

\section{Piecewise-Scaled Parametric Posteriors}
\label{sec:laplace-normal}

The ability to construct distributions viewed as Bayesian posteriors is at the heart of the methods proposed as well as the extensions that follow. To review the setup for linear regression lasso, note that the lasso can be formulated as a Bayesian regression model with a Laplace (double exponential) prior and a Normal likelihood \citep{Park2008}. In Chapter 2, we used this fact to construct intervals for the lasso from the quantiles of Laplace-Normal distributions for each $\beta_j$. In Chapter 3, slight modifications in the form of alternative Normal likelihoods were introduced which lead to intervals from distributions where the lasso estimates are the modes. Regardless, all three interval construction indicate how to define the Normal Likelihood. The extensions that follow either define an alternative prior (alternate penalties) or how to adjust the Normal likelihood (alternative outcomes). In Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal-comp} we detail in general how quantiles can be computed from these piecewise-scaled parametric distributions and use the Laplace-Normal as an example. Then, in Section~\ref{sec:look_like} we provide examples of Laplace-Normal densities. 

\subsection{Quantile Computation}
\label{sec:laplace-normal-comp}

While, the piecewise defined posteriors that are leveraged in PIPE-P and LQA-P as well as the extensions in this manuscript do not have a standard parametric form, the quantiles can still be computed analytically. 

The general outline is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define the posterior piecewise, simplifying each component to a standard parametric form and a multiplicative constant
  \item Compute the mass in each of the underlying parametric distributions in their respective intervals
  \item Use the constants in step 1 and the masses in step 2 to compute the total probability in each interval of the posterior
  \item Take the ratio of the respective components in step 2 to step 3, yielding weights
  \item Use the probabilities in step 3 and the weights in step 4 to map the probability of interest to a corresponding probability on an underlying parametric distribution
  \item Invert the CDF to compute the quantile of interest
\end{enumerate}

Now we use the Laplace-Normal distribution as a example that can be applied to all the extensions in this manuscript. The example will be described on the standard scale, but in practice all computations should occur on the log scale.

First consider a Normal likelihood parameterized as usual:
$$L(\theta | \mu, s^2) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s^2}(\theta - \mu)^2\right).$$

\noindent Then consider a Laplace prior on $\theta$:
$$p(\theta) \propto \exp(-\frac{\lam}{s^2} \abs{\theta}).$$

\noindent With this, we obtain the following Laplace-Normal distribution:
\as{
p(\theta | \mu, s^2) 
&\propto
\begin{cases}
C_{1} \exp(-\frac{1}{2s^2} (\theta_j - (\mu + \lambda))^2), \text{ if } \theta < 0, \\
C_{2} \exp(-\frac{1}{2s^2} (\theta_j - (\mu - \lambda))^2), \text{ if } \theta \geq 0 \\
\end{cases}
}

\noindent where $C_{1} = \exp(\mu \lam / s^2)$ and $C_{2} = \exp(- \mu \lam / s^2)$.

The piecewise defined distribution is made up of two scaled truncated Normal distributions. The quantiles can be obtained by mapping the probabilities of interest onto on of the respective underlying unscaled Normal distributions, $\Norm(\mu \pm \lam, s^2)$. Let $K$ be the number of pieces (here $K = 2$), and let $I_i=[\ell_i,u_i]$ be the support for piece $i$. The pieces are numbered from left to right based on their support. On their respective support, we will let the mass in the unscaled Normals be denoted $Pr_{1}$ and $Pr_{2}$ and the probability in each of the corresponding components of the Laplace-Normal be denoted $Post_{1}$ and $Post_{2}$. These can be computed stably as follows. Let $\r = \lbrace \log(C_1Pr_1), \log(C_2Pr_2) \rbrace$ and $m = \max_i r_i$, then $c = m + \log(\sum\exp(r_i - m))$ and $Post_i = \exp(r_i - c)$. Note $\sum Post_i = 1$.

With $Pr_i$ and $Post_i$ defined, we can compute a quantile by mapping the respective probability $p$ for the Laplace-Normal onto the probability $p^*$ on one of the underlying Normal distributions. The cumulative sum of $Post_i$ can be used to determine which section of the posterior the quantile comes from. For example, if $Post_1 = 0.3$ then $p = 0.1$ would have a corresponding quantile in $I_1$ but $p = 0.4$ would fall into $I_2$. Let $i^*$ denote the selected support.

Then, let $f_{i^*}(x)$ be the density for the underlying distribution in support $i^*$. The transformation to map a given probability from the Laplace-Normal depends on which support the quantile resides in: 
$$
p^* = \frac{Pr_{i^*}}{Post_{i^*}} (p - \sum_{j = 1}^{{i^*} - 1} Post_j) + \int_{-\infty}^{\ell_{i^*}} f_{i^*}(x) dx,
$$

\noindent where if $i^* = 1$ both the sum and the integral are taken to be $0$. 

The sum tells how much probability has already been accounted for in earlier regions of the posterior and the weight $Pr_{i^*} / Post_{i^*}$ indicates how much probability in the distribution transformed onto counts relative to the probability in the posterior over support $i^*$. Together, these tell how much probability is needed in distribution $i^*$ to get to the quantile. However, we need to accumulate this probability from $\ell_{i^*}$, so the integral tells us how far we need to shift over to be at $\ell_{i^*}$. Once the respective probability is mapped, we can simply use the inverse of the Normal CDF that the probability was mapped to.

Computing a quantile from any piecewise-scaled parametric distribution is outlined in Algorithm~\ref{alg:quantile-general}.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Quantile of a Piecewise-Scaled Parametric Distribution}
\label{alg:quantile-general}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require
${(C_i,F_i,F_i^{-1},I_i=[\ell_i,u_i])}_{i=1}^K, p\in(0,1)$
\Statex
\State // 1. Base (unscaled) interval masses
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $\alpha_i \gets F_i(\ell_i)$ \Comment{$\alpha_i=0$ if $\ell_i=-\infty$}
\State $\beta_i \gets F_i(u_i)$ \Comment{$\beta_i=1$ if $u_i=\infty$}
\State $Pr_i \gets \beta_i - \alpha_i$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State // 2. Normalize piece probabilities via log-sum-exp
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $r_i \gets \log C_i + \log Pr_i$
\EndFor
\State $m \gets \max_i r_i$
\State $c \gets m + \log\left(\sum_{i=1}^K \exp(r_i - m)\right)$
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $Post_i \gets \exp(r_i - c)$
\EndFor
\Statex
\State // 3. Locate active piece for probability $p$
\State $S \gets 0$
\For{$i=1$ {\bf to} $K$}
\State $S \gets S + Post_i$
\If{$p \le S$}
\State $i^\star \gets i$; \ \ $S_{prev} \gets S - Post_i$
\State \textbf{break}
\EndIf
\EndFor
\Statex
\State // 4. Map $p$ into the base CDF of piece $i^\star$ and invert
\State $w \gets Pr_{i^\star} / Post_{i^\star}$ 
\State $p^\star \gets F_{i^\star}(\ell_{i^\star}) + w \cdot (p - S_{prev})$
\State $q \gets F_{i^\star}^{-1}(p^\star)$
\State \Return $q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Figure~\ref{fig:2-man3} is a graphical representation of what is happening in the procedure above for a simple example with $\mu = 1$, $s^2 = 1$, and $\lam = 0.5$. The red and blue Normal distributions are the original Normal likelihood shifted by $\pm \lambda$. The solid portions of the Normal densities indicate the pieces of the underlying Normals that are in their respective support. The black curve is the resulting distribution which is made up of the properly normalized truncated Normals, however, this is for visualization purposes and not needed for the procedure here. Focusing on the lower bound, which in this case corresponds to $p = 0.025$, the goal is to compute the left black dashed vertical line. The blue shaded area is the corresponding probability $p^*$ and the dashed line is the lower bound computed as the quantile from the blue Normal for the probability $p^*$ which is exactly equal to the quantile corresponding to $p$ on the Laplace-Normal (the left gray shaded region).

\subsection{Laplace-Normal Densities}
\label{sec:look_like}

The shape of a Laplace-Normal distribution varies considerably based on the relative size of $\mu$ and $\lam$ which has direct implications on the resulting intervals. Intervals arising from Laplace-Normal distributions are often skewed away from zero unless $\mu$ is small or large relative to $\lam$, in which cases the intervals are more symmetric. Below is a simple setting where $s^2 = 1$, $\lam = 0.4$, and $\mu$ is set to be $0.1, 0.5$, and $1$ in the left, middle, and right panels respectively.

\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figure2-man3}
    \caption{\label{fig:1-man3} Examples of three Laplace-Normal distributions with varying likelihood means ($\mu$ = 0.1, 0.5, 1) and fixed values of $s^2 = 1$ and $\lambda = 0.4$.}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

In all three panels, the density is continuous but non-differentiable at zero. When $\mu < \lam$, the distribution has a sharp spike at zero for the mode. The larger $\mu$ is relative to $\lam$, the more the distribution resembles that of a Normal distribution. In fact, if $\mu$ is large enough relative to $\lam$, the distribution is \emph{essentially} a Normal distribution. 

\section{Conditional Normal Likelihood Estimation}
\label{sec:review}

Before outlining the extensions, we review in general the three previously introduced methods, specifically how they respectively set $\mu$ and $s^2$ in the Normal likelihoods. While the penalized regression objective does imply a likelihood, the high-dimensional likelihood is cumbersome to work with directly --- when constructing an interval for $\beta_j$, all other coefficients act as nuisance parameters. So, the methods vary in how they approximate the high-dimensional likelihood for each feature. Although the methods were introduced with lasso, they define the likelihood either independent of the penalty or in a way it can be extended to other penalties and so we present the generalized form here.

Let $\X$ be an $n \times p$ matrix of features and $\y$ be a length $n$ vector of independent outcomes. Throughout, $\X$ is standardized so that $\x_j \Tr \x_j = n$. Additionally, let $\bbh$ be the estimates for $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ using penalized regression at the value of $\lam$ which minimizes cross validation error. Let $\hat{S} = \lbrace k: \hat{\beta}_k \neq  0 \rbrace$ denote the set of selected features. Then, let $\hat{S}_j$ denote the set of selected features that excludes feature $j$, so that $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} \text{ if } j \notin \hat{S}$ and $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} - \lbrace j \rbrace \text{ if } j \in \hat{S}$. Additionally, let $\r_j = \y - \X_{-j}\bbh_{-j}$ be the partial residuals obtained by excluding feature $j$. The distinguishing features between the three methods are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:review}.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Projection} & \textbf{Mean} \\
\midrule
RL-P   & $\Q_{\hat{S}_j} = \I - \X_{\hat{S}_j}(\X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr \X_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr$ & $\tilde{\beta}_j = (\x_j \Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \x_j)^{-1} \x_j \Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \y$  \\
PIPE-P & $\Q_{\hat{S}_j} = \I - \X_{\hat{S}_j}(\X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr \X_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr$ & $z_j = \tfrac{1}{n}\x_j \Tr\r_{-j}$ \\
LQA-P  & $\tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j} = \I_n - \X_{\hat{S}_j}(\tfrac{1}{n}\X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr\X_{\hat{S}_j} + \P \W^{*-1})^{-1}\tfrac{1}{n}\X_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr$ & $z_j = \tfrac{1}{n}\x_j \Tr\r_{-j}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\label{tab:review}
\end{table}

The likelihood variance estimate for for RL-P and PIPE-P is $\hat{\sigma}^2 / \x_j \Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \x \Tr$ and for LQA-P is $\hat{\sigma}^2 / \x_j \Tr \tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j} \x \Tr$ where in both cases $\hat{\sigma}^2$, the error variance, is estimated using the suggestion of \cite{Reid2016} with $\hat{\sigma}^2 = ||\y - \X\bbh||_2^2 / (n - |\hat{S}_{\CV}|)$, where $|\hat{S}_{\CV}|$ = $\sum \left( \bbh \neq 0 \right)$.  

In summary, LQA-P and PIPE-P share the same likelihood mean while PIPE-P and RL-P share the same likelihood variance. The likelihood mean for PIPE-P and LQA-P is based on a partial projection onto the active features (i.e., uses the shrunken estimates) whereas the mean for RL-P is based on a full projection onto the active features. Meanwhile, the likelihood variance for PIPE-P and RL-P is calculated based on a full projection onto the active features whereas for LQA-P it is based on a partial projection.

\section{Bias Reducing Penalties (MCP and SCAD)}
\label{sec:bias_reducing}


A common argument against accepting intervals that have correct average coverage but vary in terms of individual coverage is that the variables that are potentially of the greatest interest (those with the largest effect) are the ones that have the lowest coverage. With that being said, this should not be counted against the proposed interval methods. This is inherently a behavior tied to the penalty used in estimation.

If this is of concern, then an alternative penalty ought to be considered. Two penalties that we discuss here are the Smoothly Clipped Absolute Deviation (SCAD) penalty and the Minimax Concave Penalty (MCP). The SCAD penalty, introduced by \cite{Fan2001}, is a concave function that heavily shrinks small coefficients while leaving sufficiently large ones essentially unpenalized, yielding an estimator with the oracle property. The MCP penalty, proposed by \cite{Zhang2010}, uses a minimax-concave piecewise quadratic-linear form, which produces nearly unbiased coefficient estimates and consistent variable selection under mild conditions. Both penalties have a tuning parameter, $\gamma$, which controls the amount of curvature. The SCAD and MCP penalties are defined as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{SCAD \;(\(\gamma>2\))} &:\qquad
\displaystyle
p_\lambda\!\bigl(|\beta|;\,\lambda,\gamma\bigr)=
\begin{cases}
\lambda|\beta|, &
0 \le |\beta| \le \lambda, \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2\gamma\lambda|\beta| - |\beta|^{2} - \lambda^{2}}
      {2(\gamma-1)}, &
\lambda < |\beta| < \gamma\lambda, \\[10pt]
\dfrac{\lambda^{2}(\gamma+1)}{2}, &
|\beta| \ge \gamma\lambda
\end{cases} \\[12pt]
\textbf{MCP \;(\(\gamma>1\))} &:\qquad
\displaystyle
p_\lambda\!\bigl(|\beta|;\,\lambda,\gamma\bigr)=
\begin{cases}
\lambda|\beta| - \dfrac{|\beta|^{2}}{2\gamma}, &
0 \le |\beta| \le \gamma\lambda, \\[6pt]
\dfrac{\gamma\lambda^{2}}{2}, &
|\beta| \ge \gamma\lambda.
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$


\noindent Both SCAD and MCP are single-stage approaches that taper off penalization as $\beta$ increases in magnitude. 

Figure~\ref{fig:4-man3} displays what these penalties look like in comparison to the lasso penalty. For SCAD, $\gam$  is set to be $3.7$ and for MCP $\gam$ is set to be $3$, which are both commonly used defaults in practice. The same value of $\lam$ is used for all three penalties for consistency, however, the $\lam$ used for each penalty in practice generally differs even when applied to the same dataset.


\begin{figure}[hbt!]
    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figure3-man3}
    \caption{\label{fig:4-man3} A depiction of the lasso, MCP, and SCAD penalties as a function of $\beta$. For all 3 penalties, $\lam = 1$. For MCP, $\gamma = 3$ and for SCAD, $\gamma = 3.7$.}
    \end{center}
\end{figure}

These penalties are often referred to as non-convex penalties with $\gam$ determining the rate the penalty tapers off at (and consequently, the concavity). Both penalties start out by applying the same rate of penalization as the lasso but then smoothly relax the penalization rate to zero as the magnitude of the estimate increases. The main difference between MCP and SCAD is that MCP relaxes the penalization immediately while for SCAD the penalization rate remains flat until the estimate exceeds $\lam$.

\subsection{Overview}

Computation of the intervals for SCAD and MCP proceed exactly as described in Algorithm~\ref{alg:quantile-general}. What remains to be worked out is the piecewise defined distributions. Here, as in Section~\ref{sec:laplace-normal-comp}, the details are provided in general for any Normal likelihood.

Priors corresponding to penalties are proportional to $\exp(-p_\lam(\theta) / s^2)$, where $p_\lam(\theta)$ is the respective penalty. This leads to a piecewise defined posterior for SCAD of

\[
p(\theta \mid \mu, s^2)
\;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
  C_{1}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta < -\,\gamma\lambda,\\[2ex]

  C_{2}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu + \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
            {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
  \Bigg),
  & -\gamma\lambda \le \theta < -\lambda,\\[3ex]

  C_{3}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-(\mu+\lambda))^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & -\lambda \le \theta < 0,\\[2ex]

  C_{4}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-(\mu-\lambda))^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & 0 \le \theta \le \lambda,\\[2ex]

  C_{5}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu - \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
            {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
  \Bigg),
  & \lambda < \theta \le \gamma\lambda,\\[3ex]

  C_{6}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta > \gamma\lambda,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\text{with constants}\qquad
\begin{aligned}
C_{1} = C_{6}
&= \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\gamma+1)\lambda^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1ex]
C_{2}
&= \exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{\displaystyle \Big(\dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu + \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
         {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
    - \frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}
    + \frac{\lambda^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}
  \Bigg),\\[2ex]
C_{3}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{(\mu+\lambda)^2 - \mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\qquad
C_{4}
= \exp\!\Big(\frac{(\mu-\lambda)^2 - \mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1ex]
C_{5}
&= \exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{\displaystyle \Big(\dfrac{(\gamma-1)\mu - \gamma\lambda}{\gamma-2}\Big)^{\!2}}
         {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma-1}{\gamma-2}}
    - \frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}
    + \frac{\lambda^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}
  \Bigg).
\end{aligned}
\]

\noindent For MCP the piecewise defined distribution is

\[
p(\theta \mid \mu, s^2)
\;\propto\;
\begin{cases}
  C_{1}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta < -\,\gamma\lambda,\\[2.0ex]

  C_{2}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\,\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(\mu+\lambda)\Big)^{\!2}}
             {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}
  \Bigg),
  & -\gamma\lambda \le \theta < 0,\\[3.0ex]

  C_{3}\;
  \exp\!\Bigg(
    -\,\dfrac{\Big(\theta - \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(\mu-\lambda)\Big)^{\!2}}
             {2\,s^2 \dfrac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}
  \Bigg),
  & 0 \le \theta \le \gamma\lambda,\\[3.0ex]

  C_{4}\;
  \exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\theta-\mu)^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),
  & \theta > \gamma\lambda,
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\text{with constants}\qquad
\begin{aligned}
C_{1} = C_4
&= \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\gamma\,\lambda^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1.0ex]
C_{2}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{\gamma\,(\mu+\lambda)^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}\;-\;\frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big),\\[1.0ex]
C_{3}
&= \exp\!\Big(\frac{\gamma\,(\mu-\lambda)^2}{2(\gamma-1)\,s^2}\;-\;\frac{\mu^2}{2\,s^2}\Big).
\end{aligned}
\]

\subsection{Results}

The WHO/ARI Multicentre Study collected data on several acute respiratory illnesses in multiple countries. Here, we analyze a subset of this study, concerning 816 infants who presented with symptoms of pneumonia in Ethiopia, a major cause of morbidity and mortality for infants under 3 months of age. \subsection{Revisiting an argument for biased intervals}
\label{sec:bias_v_debias}

The introduction of bias reducing penalties brings in an interesting addition to the biased versus debiased interval discussion. With SCAD and MCP, there is perhaps less of a benefit to debiasing as such penalties attempt to introduce the minimum amount of bias necessary. To this end, consider the following simulation comparing PIPE-P with lasso, SCAD, and MCP penalties to debiased methods. Results are provided in Table~\ref{tab:1-man3}. The coverages and widths in the table are for the features selected by the respective penalties in each iteration of the simulation. We refer to this coverage as the relevant average coverage as it focuses on the features which are presumably of greatest interest to the practitioner: those selected by the respective penalty. Iterations where the penalty selected no variables are excluded from the table.

  \item $p = 100$: $4$ large signals ($\beta = \pm 5$)

\section{-Net Penalties}
\label{sec:enet}

Proposed by \cite{zou2005regularization}, the Elastic Net blends lasso's sparsity with Ridge's shrinkage to retain groups of highly correlated predictors while controlling estimation variability. The Elastic Net penalty is a mixture of $L_2$ (ridge) and $L_1$ (lasso) penalties:
$$
p_\lam(\bb | \lam, \alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha) \lam }{2} ||\bb||^2_2 + \alpha \lam ||\bb||_1,
$$

\noindent where $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ controls the tradeoff between the two components. When $\alpha = 1$, the penalty becomes the lasso penalty and when $\alpha = 0$, the penalty becomes the ridge penalty. The main reason one may choose to use Elastic Net over the lasso is that the addition of the ridge penalty can add stability. So while the previous methods in Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing} focused on reducing bias, here the addition of the $L_2$ component reduces the variability compared to the lasso estimates. Furthermore, while the lasso solutions are not always unique, Elastic Net solutions are, a quality inherited from the ridge penalty. However, as mentioned, Elastic Net still results in sparsity, a quality inherited from the lasso.

The Ridge penalty can also be applied to the nonconvex penalties. Actually, there may be an even stronger motivation as the objective functions for MCP and SCAD may fail to be convex (resulting in multiple local minima). Introducing a Ridge penalty (which is strictly convex) can help improve numerical stability.

So, the general form for -Net type penalties is

$$
p_\lam(\bb | \lam, \alpha) = \frac{(1 - \alpha) \lam }{2} ||\bb||^2_2 + p_{\lam}(\bb | \lam^* = \alpha\lam, \ldots),
$$

\noindent where $p_{\lam}(\bb | \lam^* = \alpha\lam, \ldots)$ is the penalty for one of the previously described sparse penalties (lasso, SCAD, MCP) but with $\lam$ adjusted based on the relative contribution of that sparse penalty. 

\subsection{Overview}

The prior corresponding to -Net penalty for a single $\beta_j$ is

$$
\propto \exp(-\frac{(1-\alpha) \lam}{2s^2}\beta_j^2)\exp(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \lam^* = \alpha\lam, \ldots)),
$$

\noindent With this prior, the posterior can be generally defined as

\as{
p(\beta | \mu, s^2) &\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2s^2}(\beta_j - \mu)^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(1-\alpha) \lam}{2s^2}\beta_j^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \lam^* = \alpha\lam, \ldots)\right) \\
&\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1 + (1-\alpha)\lam}{2s^2}(\beta_j - \frac{\mu}{1 + (1 - \alpha)\lam})^2\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{s^2} p_{\lam}(\bb | \lam^* = \alpha\lam, \ldots)\right).
}

\noindent That is, the Ridge portion of the prior can be absorbed into the Normal likelihood resulting in an updated mean and variance. From here, the process proceeds along the lines of the respective sparse penalty in Section~\ref{sec:bias_reducing}.

\subsection{Results}

We would expect the Elastic Net intervals to have behavior that falls into a middle ground between Ridge Posterior intervals and PIPE Posterior intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:6-man3} provides a comparison between intervals produced by Ridge Posterior, those produced by lasso (PIPE-P), and those produced by Elastic Net (PIPE-P with $\alpha = 0.5$). The data for the simulation was generated with $n = p = 100$, $\beta_{A} = 1$ and $\beta_{B}, \beta_{N1}, \ldots, \beta_{N98} = 0$, $\cor(\x_{A}, \x_{B}) = .99$, while all of the N (noise) variables are uncorrelated with $A$, $B$, and each other, $\X \iid \Norm(0,1)$, and $\y = \X\bb + \epsilon$, $\epsilon \iid \Norm(0,1)$. This simulation setup is illuminating in understand how different methods handle uncertainty, here in the form of high correlation between two predictors.

The stability introduced by the ridge penalty reduces the selection-driven polarization in interval widths compared to using the lasso alone, yielding greater consistency both across simulations and features A and B. This occurs because with Elastic Net the highly correlated features $A$ and $B$ are almost always selected together.

It should be noted that Ridge Posterior serves as a general reference here. Even if $\alpha$ is near 0, the resulting intervals for Elastic Net with PIPE-P will be be different. In fact, they are expected to be considerably different because PIPE-P is still using an approximation to the high-dimensional Normal likelihood with a full projection based variance. As $\alpha$ is decreased towards $0$ the impact of the penalty will resemble the Ridge penalty, but the variance will be heavily inflated due to the lack of sparsity. On the other hand, LQA-P intervals (\ref{sup:lqa}) have more similar behavior to Ridge Posterior.

\section{Generalized Linear Models}
\label{sec:alt-fam}

While continuous outcomes are often the starting point for methodological development, they are far from the only outcome type encountered in biomedical research. In fact, binary outcomes analyzed with logistic regression and count outcomes modeled with Poisson regression are arguably even more pervasive. In this section, we extend the methods to logistic and Poisson lasso penalized regression by detailing the resulting adjustments to both the Normal likelihood and the prior. For $\y$ a length $n$ vector of independent outcomes, $\X$ an $n \times p + 1$ matrix of predictors, $\bb$ a length $p + 1$ vector of coefficients, and a general penalty with $\lambda>0$, the penalized objectives are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Logistic:}\quad
Q_{\text{bern}}(\bb\mid\X,\y,\lambda)
  &= \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
     \Bigl[
       \log\!\bigl(1+e^{\X_i^{\!\top}\bb}\bigr)
       - y_i\,\X_i^{\!\top}\bb
     \Bigr]
     + p_\lam(\bb|\lam, \ldots), \\[6pt]
\text{Poisson:}\quad
Q_{\text{pois}}(\bb\mid\X,\y,\lambda)
  &= \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
     \Bigl[
       e^{\X_i^{\!\top}\bb}
       - y_i\,\X_i^{\!\top}\bb
     \Bigr]
     + p_\lam(\bb|\lam, \ldots).
\end{aligned}
$$

\noindent The design includes an intercept whereas for gaussian outcomes, $\y$ was assumed centered and as such no intercept term was needed. However, note that the intercept does not get penalized.

\subsection{Overview}

Many of the details for fitting penalized regression to generalized outcomes are what are needed to extend the methods. Coordinate descent algorithms for getting estimates for Poisson and logistic penalized regression use Taylor series expansions to obtain quadratic approximations to the loss function and then proceed with the same algorithm used for linear penalized regression. Regardless of the family, the general form for the Taylor series approximated loss is as follows.

Let $\boldsymbol{\eta} = \X \bb$, then
$$
L(\bb|\X,\y) \approx \tfrac{1}{2n} (\tilde{\y} - \boldsymbol{\eta}) \Tr \W (\tilde{\y} - \boldsymbol{\eta}),
$$

\noindent where $\tilde{\y} = \hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}} + \W^{-1}(\y - \hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}})$. What changes from family to family is just $\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$ and $\W$ which for binomial and Poisson families are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat{\pi}_i &=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle
\frac{e^{\hat{\eta}_i}}{1+e^{\hat{\eta}_i}}
  & \text{(Binomial)}\\[10pt]
\displaystyle
e^{\hat{\eta}_i}
  & \text{(Poisson)}
\quad\quad\quad\quad
\end{cases}, \text{ and }
\mathbf W &=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\hat{\pi}_i(1-\hat{\pi}_i)\bigr)
  & \text{(Binomial)}\\[10pt]
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\hat{\pi}_i\bigr)
  & \text{(Poisson)}
\end{cases}.
\end{aligned}
$$


\noindent This process can be applied to other GLMs by deriving their respective values for $\hat{\pi}_i$ and $\W$. The objective function resembles that of linear penalized regression, but with observational weights, $\W$. 

Accordingly, $\tilde{z}_j = \frac{1}{n}\x_j \Tr\boldsymbol{W} (\tilde{\y} -  \X_{-j} \bbh_{-j})$ is the weighted regression of the partial pseudo residuals $(\tilde{\y} -  \X_{-j} \bbh_{-j})$ onto $\x_j$. Conditional on $\bb_{-j} = \bbh_{-j}$, the mean for $\beta_j$ = $\tilde{z}_j / \nu_j$ where $\nu_j = \frac{1}{n} \x \Tr_j \boldsymbol{W} \x_j$.

Similarly, the projection matrices are also weighted. Let $\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} = \W^{1/2} \X_{\hat{S}_j}$. Then the full projection matrix onto the active set (for PIPE-P) is
$$
\Q_{\hat{S}_j} = \I - \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j}(\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr.
$$

Of course, unlike the gaussian case, we do not have an error variance as the variance is not independent of the mean. So, for feature $j$, the variance used in the Normal likelihood is $(\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2}\x_j)^{-1}$. For LQA-P, $\tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j}$ is used instead of $\Q_{\hat{S}_j}$ where
$$\tilde{\Q}_{\hat{S}_j} = \I_n - \tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j}(\tfrac{1}{n}\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} + \P\W^{*-1})^{-1}\tfrac{1}{n}\tilde{\X}_{\hat{S}_j} \Tr.$$

\noindent To ensure the modes match the corresponding point estimates, $\lam$ must also be scaled for each parameter:
$$
\lambda_j = \frac{\lambda}{\nu_j}.
$$

\noindent However, for -Net type penalties, scaling is not applied to the ridge portion. That is, letting $\lam_1 = \alpha \lam$ and $\lam_2$ = $(1-\alpha)\lam$, then 

$$
\lambda_{1,j} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\nu_j},
$$

\noindent and $\lam_2$ is left alone.

With these pieces in place, the process to compute intervals proceeds as usual.


\subsection{Results}

The coverage patterns for binary and count outcomes are similar to the pattern for gaussian outcomes: higher coverage near zero, lower coverage away from zero, and average coverage converging to the nominal rate. Examples for logistic and Poisson penalized regression are provided in Figure~\ref{fig:3-man3}. Each data set was simulated as follows. The elements of $\X_{n \times p}$ were generated independently from a $\Norm(0, 1)$ with $p = 101$ and four different values of $n$: 50, 100, 400, and 1000. Additionally, $\bb$ was set to equally spaced quantiles of a Laplace distribution. The coefficients were then scaled so that the latent signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) equals 1. Finally, $\y$ was generated as $\y = \Pois(\exp(\X\bb))$ for the Poisson outcome and $\y = \Bern(\logit^{-1}(\X\bb))$ for a binary outcome. For both Poisson and logistic penalized regression, we generated 1000 independent data sets for each value of n; for each data set, PIPE-P intervals with a lasso penalty were constructed and coverage determined. The dashed lines represent the average coverage for each method across all coefficients, while the solid lines are smoothed estimates of coverage as a function of $\beta$.

    \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figure6-man3}

While the overall coverage patterns for binary and count outcomes are similar to the coverage pattern for gaussian outcomes, they differ by outcome type due to different likelihood variance calculations and the relative amounts of information per observation in the different outcome types. For logistic and Poisson regression, the variance is not modeled independent of the mean, and, as such, the computation of the likelihood variance differs by outcome type. For a gaussian outcome, the method recommended by \cite{Reid2016} is used to estimate error variance which is then used in the estimate for the likelihood variance. For Poisson and logistic penalized regression, the estimate of likelihood variance is instead $(\x_j \Tr \W^{1/2} \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \W^{1/2}\x_j)^{-1}$. And, while the form is the same for logistic and Poisson penalized regression, they of course differ due to their respective $\W$ matrices. Lastly, it is well known that the amount of information per n is lower for binary outcomes which likely accounts for the lower coverage for larger coefficient values and the wider window of over coverage around zero compared to the Poisson outcome at the same values of n.

\section{Discussion}

The extensions in this manuscript share the same objective as the original methods: to produce a single, coherent set of coefficient estimates, predictions, and intervals. The extensions are implemented in the function \texttt{intervals} as part of the \texttt{ncvreg} package and provide full coverage for the range of outcome / penalty combinations available to fit with the function \texttt{ncvreg}. In total, there are over 50 combinations, so deeper exploration of the coverage behaviors is an ongoing endeavor.

% Additionally, \texttt{ncvreg} supports the use of penalty factors which is not handled in the current implementation of \texttt{intervals}, although this extension in theory is trivial.

% Average coverage in part arises from higher coverage for small parameters, many of which have estimates equal to zero (i.e. not selected). Some feel that such features should be considered ``confident nulls'' and that intervals have little utility for these features. However, we would argue that constructing intervals can be extremely informative. Considering Figure~\ref{fig:6-man3}, one can see why treating all predictors not selected as the same is short sighted. In this example, the N variables all have rather narrow intervals indicating confidence in the lack of selection whereas variable B on the other hand has an extremely wide interval. Likewise, intervals may often be skewed away from zero which again can be informative even if the original estimate was equal to zero.
